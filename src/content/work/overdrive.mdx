## Overview

OVERDRIVE is an arena shooter in the spirit of ULTRAKILL: dashing, sliding, a grapple hook, parrying, several weapons and a style meter that rewards playing aggressively. There are five ways to play. **Arena** runs four arenas (a sunset yard, a foundry with lava channels, a tower you have to climb, and a night-time reactor) of three waves each, against nine enemy types that each demand a different answer, then a boss, and then the Sanctum: a huge arena under an eclipse where you duel the final boss, a sword-wielding knight. **Fast** is the Gauntlet, a time trial through seven rooms joined by boost tubes that fire you along at 26 m/s, with a run clock, splits against your best run and par ranks. **Endless** throws generated waves at you in the reactor until you die, and the **Daily** challenge is one endless run a day that's the same for everyone, its arena, waves and a modifier picked from the date. Killing the final boss unlocks **Act II**, a second campaign beneath the first that's in preview as I build it: you fall into a flooded cathedral where the water slows you on foot (slide to skim across it) and rises every wave until only the galleries stay dry, then descend into an orrery where two rings of platforms turn over a captive sun whose flare sweeps the arena. Below that a cage rides down a vast shaft, stopping at a different floor for each wave, to a pit where **the Penitent** kneels, chained to the walls: shoot its six anchors out or grapple onto them and rip them free, jump its low censer sweeps and slide under the high ones, and when the last chain breaks it stands up and comes for you. Its enemies are new too: a winged Seraph that sweeps a beam after you, an Anchor whose field stops you dashing or grappling, and Hollowed variants of the old ones (enraged, splitting in two when they die, or haloed against everything but a headshot or a parry). Waves aren't always "kill everything": some ask you to hold a zone, destroy the pylons the enemies pour out of, or survive a reactor overload, and the arenas change as a fight goes on (the sun sets, the lava rises, the platforms speed up). Every run is scored on style, speed and the damage you avoided, and a good enough score puts your name on that mode's leaderboard. Kills earn XP, scaled by how stylishly you play, and levels buy weapon upgrades for a revolver, a pump shotgun, a bolt rifle (the Lancer) and a heavy sniper rifle. The four are built as one arsenal: blackened metal and bone, each glowing its own colour, with cells on the gun that show the rounds left. Four difficulties change how hard enemies hit, how fast they fire and how far they lead their shots, and the soundtrack is synthesized live, building from the tubes into each fight. Sound effects go through the game's own mixer: enemies, doors and blasts sound from where they happen (panned, quieter and darker with distance), the music ducks when you're hit or a boss winds up, and every arena has its own reverb. Every enemy has a voice of its own, a machine body carrying a hollow choir, with a different wind-up sound for each attack, so you can hear what's about to hit you even when it's behind a wall.

There's no engine underneath. The game is about 20,000 lines of C++17 plus about 620 lines of GLSL on top of SDL2 for windowing and input, OpenGL 3.3 Core for rendering and GLM for math. It builds with a Makefile on macOS, on Windows through MSYS2, and for the browser with Emscripten, which is the build running above. I built it to learn what an engine actually does for you, and the answer turned out to be mostly **time**: deciding when things happen matters more than how they're drawn.

Game code is organised as states (menu, gameplay) that own their systems. Collision is deliberately simple: the player and every wall or platform are axis-aligned boxes, overlaps are resolved by pushing the player out along the axis of least penetration, and a spatial grid, rebuilt whenever the level's walls change, keeps each query to nearby geometry.

## Hard problems

### Physics that doesn't care about your frame rate

Movement in a game like this is tuned by feel: jump height, dash distance, how much speed a grapple builds. If physics runs once per rendered frame, all of that changes with the monitor. A 144 Hz display jumps differently from a 60 Hz one. So simulation runs on a **fixed 1/60 s step**, fed by an accumulator of real elapsed time:

<CodeRef lang="cpp" repo="https://github.com/Ollie1o1/3d_shooter" file="src/GameplayState.h" lines="1283-1318" code={"Uint64 now = SDL_GetPerformanceCounter();\ndouble elapsed = g_fixedDt > 0.0 ? g_fixedDt : (double)(now - prevTicks) / (double)freq;\nprevTicks = now;\nif (g_devCam && g_devNoMouse) devCamera((float)elapsed);\naccumulator += elapsed;\nif (accumulator > 0.25) accumulator = 0.25;\n\n// ... read the keyboard, and a gamepad folded into the same keys ...\n\nwhile (accumulator >= PHYSICS_DT) {\n    prevCamPos = player.camera.position;\n    snapshotForInterpolation();\n    if (hitStopFrames > 0) {\n        --hitStopFrames;  // freeze simulation, consume one tick\n    } else {\n        physicsTick(PHYSICS_DT, keys, parryKey);\n    }\n    accumulator -= PHYSICS_DT;\n}"} />

(`g_fixedDt` is the footage mode: when recording the showcase video, every frame advances exactly 1/30 s however long it took to render.) Two details do a lot of work here. The accumulator is **clamped at 0.25 s**, so a stall (a window drag, a breakpoint) can't make the game try to catch up on hundreds of ticks at once, the classic "spiral of death". And **hit-stop**, the brief freeze when a hit lands, is implemented by consuming ticks without simulating, so it's frame-rate independent for free.

Running physics at 60 Hz on a 144 Hz screen would look like judder, so rendering **interpolates** between the last two physics states using whatever fraction of a tick is left in the accumulator. The camera, every enemy, projectile and pickup, and the moving platforms all keep their previous position for this, so the game is smooth at 240 or 360 Hz too:

<CodeRef lang="cpp" repo="https://github.com/Ollie1o1/3d_shooter" file="src/GameplayState.h" lines="3052-3058" code={"void render() override {\n    applyTextureQuality();\n    float alpha = (float)(accumulator / PHYSICS_DT);\n    renderAlpha = glm::clamp(alpha, 0.f, 1.f);\n    glm::vec3 renderCamPos = glm::mix(prevCamPos, player.camera.position, alpha);\n    Camera renderCam = player.camera;\n    renderCam.position = renderCamPos;"} />

### Movement that rewards skill

The obvious implementation, `velocity = direction * speed`, feels dead: no momentum, and nothing to master. OVERDRIVE uses Quake's acceleration model instead. It projects the current velocity onto the direction you're pressing and only adds speed up to a cap **along that direction**:

<CodeRef lang="cpp" repo="https://github.com/Ollie1o1/3d_shooter" file="src/Player.h" lines="247-265" code={"// WASD acceleration \u2014 suppressed while sliding so momentum carries.\nif (!sliding) {\n    glm::vec3 wishDir{0.f};\n    if (keys[SDL_SCANCODE_W]) wishDir += flatFwd;\n    if (keys[SDL_SCANCODE_S]) wishDir -= flatFwd;\n    if (keys[SDL_SCANCODE_D]) wishDir += flatRight;\n    if (keys[SDL_SCANCODE_A]) wishDir -= flatRight;\n\n    float accel = onGround ? acceleration : airAcceleration;\n\n    if (glm::length(wishDir) > 0.001f) {\n        wishDir = glm::normalize(wishDir);\n        glm::vec3 hVel{velocity.x, 0.f, velocity.z};\n        float currentSpeed = glm::dot(hVel, wishDir);\n        float addSpeed = glm::clamp(horizontalSpeed - currentSpeed, 0.f, accel * dt);\n        velocity.x += wishDir.x * addSpeed;\n        velocity.z += wishDir.z * addSpeed;\n    }\n}"} />

Because the cap is on the *projection*, turning the camera while strafing in the air keeps finding directions where `currentSpeed` is below the cap, so a skilled player can gain speed beyond the normal maximum. That's the same mechanic behind strafe-jumping in Quake and the movement feel of Titanfall and ULTRAKILL. Air acceleration is about half of ground acceleration (42 vs 80), friction drops to a quarter during a slide, and it's skipped entirely while grappling so the hook can build speed. Getting the grapple and dash to feel right took five separate commits of tuning and fixes.

### Encounters you can read

An early version had cube enemies wandering two huge rooms, and it wasn't fun: you couldn't tell enemies apart at a distance, couldn't see an attack coming, and spent most of the time looking for the last one. The redesign started from what each enemy should *ask of the player*. A Husk fires slow orbs you can parry; a Ripper crouches before it lunges, so you dash; a Sentinel paints you with a laser before its burst, so you break line of sight; a Brute slams the ground, so you jump the shockwave; a Mite explodes, so you shoot it early and its blast hurts its friends instead; a Shieldbearer's tower shield stops bullets from the front, so you flank it (it turns slowly), shoot over the rim, or parry its bash.

That only works if the player can read it, so every enemy is a rig of boxes on joints (hips, shoulders, wing roots), posed from its AI state each frame. Legs swing with the walk cycle, a Raptor's wings flap, and arms come up to aim or overhead to slam during a wind-up. Each rig is 11 to 30 boxes, and all of them, plus death debris, projectiles, doors and jump pads, go to the GPU as one instanced draw call.

Waves are run by a small director that keeps 6 to 10 enemies on the field and trickles the rest in, away from the player:

<CodeRef lang="cpp" repo="https://github.com/Ollie1o1/3d_shooter" file="src/WaveDirector.h" lines="105-111" code={"} else if (!queue.empty() && aliveCount < maxAlive() && spawnTimer <= 0.f) {\n    Queued q = queue.front();\n    queue.erase(queue.begin());\n    out.push_back({q.type, q.fixed ? q.pos : pickSpawn(q.type, playerPos)});\n    spawnTimer = SPAWN_GAP;\n    ++aliveCount;   // it's on the field now; don't call the wave clear this tick\n}"} />

That last line is a bug fix. Without it, the wave-clear check right below compared against the alive count from *before* the spawn, so a wave whose last enemy spawned into an empty field ended instantly. The boss wave is a single enemy, so it always did. The director and the enemy AI have no OpenGL in them, which is what made this findable: a headless test drives the director through an entire simulated run, and a regression test now holds the boss wave open while the boss is alive.

### Climbing, and a time trial

The fourth arena, the Spire, is built around height: a 26 m tower with ledges, bridges, a balcony and a summit. Each wave spawns a tier higher, and the riflemen up there won't step off their perch (a ledge check in their steering treats a drop like a wall), so you have to climb to them. Rippers and Mites have no such check and jump down after you.

Lifts, sweepers and orbiting platforms connect the tiers, and you can grapple any of them. They needed almost no new machinery: a moving platform is an ordinary wall flagged as dynamic. It moves every physics tick and records how far it went. It's kept out of the spatial grid and tested every tick instead. The player remembers which wall it is standing on, and riding a platform is just adding that platform's movement before the physics step. A test sweeps every platform through two full cycles and fails if any of them ever passes through a wall.

Fast mode reuses the same wave director with a flag. Its first version was a straight run of sections, and it played like a hallway. The second made each section a long channel with a breather before the fight, which paced better but still read as one corridor. The current version, the Gauntlet, is built as **rooms joined by boost tubes**. A tube is the breather: health waiting, nothing shooting, and a ribbed tunnel that fires you along at 26 m/s. Doors split open as you sprint at them, lock behind you when a room's fight starts, and the exit unlocks when the room is clear. The seven rooms are a canal between raised walkways, five terraces climbing 20 m, islands and ferries over a void, a tower you fall through floor by floor, a pump room opening into a hall with galleries, and a courtyard with a lift shaft up to the beacon. Tests check that no enemy is placed in a tube, that every fight waits for its room, that the route really does change direction and height, and that every placed enemy appears exactly once, exactly where it was placed.

### An enemy worth parrying

Parrying was in from the start, but nothing made it necessary. The Juggernaut is built around it: its armor halves bullet damage, its slow siege shells come back for 400 if you parry them, and punching its overhead smash in the last 0.4 s of the wind-up breaks it, leaving it staggered and taking double damage. The parry itself became a visible punch with a clank, a flash and a hit-stop, and its window now scales with the speed of the shot, so a sniper round is as fair to parry as a slow orb.

### A final boss built from wind-ups

The Warden, the first boss, is a damage sponge with volleys and summons. The final boss needed to be a duel, so the Sovereign is a 3.5 m knight with a greatsword whose whole moveset is built from readable **wind-ups**. He dashes at you at 34 m/s, often dashing again straight out of the first one. Up close he chains a sweep each way into an overhead cleave whose shockwave you have to jump. If you climb to high ground he leaps up after you on a ballistic arc, solved each time for where you're standing. At range he throws fans of crescent slashes you can parry. Each attack has its own held pose (the blade drawn back low for a dash, wound to one side for a sweep, raised overhead for the cleave) and a follow-through, and each one is driven by explicit joint angles layered over the shared humanoid rig. The answer to all of it is the parry: punch a sweep or the cleave in its last quarter second and his guard breaks.

He fights in the Sanctum, an arena more than twice the size of any before it, built for movement: an open floor to dash around him, a ring of 18 m pillars to grapple, raised corners and floating islands reached by jump pads, and platforms orbiting the middle. Tests drive him against a player who stands still (he connects 25 times in 25 seconds), one who kites him in a circle (he closes the gap with chained dashes) and one standing on an 11 m island (he lands on it).

### Headshots that match the model

Shots that clearly hit an enemy in the head kept doing body damage, and the Juggernaut was the worst case. There were two causes. The Juggernaut had simply been left off the list of enemies with heads. And for everyone else, a headshot was decided by **where the bullet entered the enemy's body box**: above 80% of its height counted as the head. A body box is as wide as the shoulders, so when you aim up at a 3 m enemy, the ray enters the front face of the box well below the head it is about to hit. The reverse happened too: a Brute's shoulder pads sat inside its "head" band.

Now each enemy has a real head hitbox, built from the same proportions as its model, so the head you see is the head you hit. The humanoids share a table of body measurements (leg length, torso height, head size) that both the rig and the hitbox read. The box follows the model's animation: walking bob, a broken Juggernaut slumping forward, a Ripper's crouch before its lunge, a Raptor pitching into a dive. Because the box is rotated with the enemy's facing, the code transforms its corners into world space and takes the axis-aligned box around them. It is slightly larger than the drawn head so a shot that grazes it still counts. A shot is a headshot when the ray passes through that box before any wall, even where the head pokes out above the body box. A headshot now also flashes the hitmarker gold, so you know it landed. Tests fire rays from eye height at every humanoid's head and chest and check which ones count.

### A renderer with no art assets

There isn't a single image file in the repository. Floor grids, brick walls, metal ceilings and crates are generated at startup from value noise and uploaded as textures:

<CodeRef lang="cpp" repo="https://github.com/Ollie1o1/3d_shooter" file="src/TextureGen.h" lines="22-35" code={"inline float hash(float x, float y) {\n    float h = sinf(x * 12.9898f + y * 78.233f) * 43758.5453f;\n    return h - floorf(h);\n}\n\ninline float smoothNoise(float x, float y) {\n    float ix = floorf(x), iy = floorf(y);\n    float fx = x - ix, fy = y - iy;\n    fx = fx * fx * (3.f - 2.f * fx);\n    fy = fy * fy * (3.f - 2.f * fy);\n    float a = hash(ix, iy), b = hash(ix+1, iy);\n    float c = hash(ix, iy+1), d = hash(ix+1, iy+1);\n    return a + (b-a)*fx + (c-a)*fy + (a-b-c+d)*fx*fy;\n}"} />

The frame renders to an offscreen framebuffer, with all enemies in a single instanced draw call, then goes through bloom: a bright-pass extract followed by **ten** separable Gaussian blur passes that ping-pong between two framebuffers, alternating horizontal and vertical:

<CodeRef lang="cpp" repo="https://github.com/Ollie1o1/3d_shooter" file="src/PostProcess.h" lines="136-145" code={"bool horizontal = true;\nfor (int i=0;i<10;++i) {\n    glBindFramebuffer(GL_FRAMEBUFFER,bloomFBO[horizontal?1:0]);\n    blurShader.use();\n    blurShader.setInt(\"image\",0);\n    blurShader.setInt(\"horizontal\",horizontal?1:0);\n    glBindTexture(GL_TEXTURE_2D,bloomTex[horizontal?0:1]);\n    drawQuad();\n    horizontal = !horizontal;\n}"} />

The optional CRT pass adds barrel distortion, chromatic aberration and scanlines on top, before the HUD and the weapon view model are drawn.

Everything renders into a 16:9 canvas at a fixed resolution and is then scaled into the window, letterboxed if needed, while the HUD lays itself out in a virtual 1280×720. That makes resolution a single number, and the **graphics quality** setting picks it: 720p, 1080p, 1440p or 4K. Rendering above the screen's own resolution is supersampling, which smooths edges. Above the lowest setting the world textures also get anisotropic filtering, so floors stay sharp where they stretch into the distance. On an M2 MacBook a frame takes about 1.7 ms at 720p, 3.8 ms at 1440p and 6 ms at 4K.

### Sound from somewhere

For most of its life the game played every sound effect the same way: through SDL_mixer's channels, at a fixed volume, in both ears. An enemy winding up behind you sounded exactly like one in front of you, and a few call sites faked distance by turning their own volume down. So the effects now go through a mixer of my own, running in the same audio callback that already synthesizes the music. It has 48 voices in four groups (the player, enemies, the world, the UI), each with a cap, so a crowd of dying enemies can't drown out your own gun or the boss. A sound placed in the world falls off with distance, pans toward its side and gets darker through a one-pole low-pass, both with distance and when it's behind you. Every play varies its pitch and level a little so ten shots don't sound like one sample repeated, the music and the world duck for a moment when you're hit or a boss winds up, and each arena feeds a small Freeverb-style reverb tuned to its space: dry in the open yard, metallic in the foundry, long and dark in the cathedral. The game thread never touches the voices; it pushes commands into a lock-free single-producer ring that the audio thread drains at the top of each buffer, so nothing on the audio thread locks or allocates.

The mixer has no SDL in it, so the tests render it offline and measure the result: a sound on your right is louder in the right channel, the same sound behind you has less high-frequency energy, a full group gives up its quietest voice, and silence in gives exactly silence out. A code review then caught the classic reverb bug. After a sound dies away, a feedback delay line decays toward zero but never reaches it: the values sink into denormal floats, the smallest the hardware can represent, where multiplying by the feedback rounds back to the same number. Apple Silicon handles denormals at full speed, so nothing showed up in a benchmark on my laptop, but on many x86 CPUs arithmetic on them is many times slower, and WebAssembly can't switch them off, so in the browser that cost could have eaten into frame time from the first gunshot onward. The filters now flush anything below 10⁻¹⁵ to zero, and a test renders a minute of silence after a sound and counts the denormals left in the reverb: zero.


### Enemies you can hear coming

Until recently every enemy in the game made the same sound: one shared beep when any of them wound up an attack, one death sound, one spawn sound. A Husk about to fire sounded exactly like a Juggernaut about to fire a siege shell. Now each of the fifteen enemy types has its own voice, in the game's "dark machine" style: a machine body (servos, hydraulics, gear grind, treads, rattling chains) carrying a hollow choir of synthesized vowels. Size sets the pitch, so a Mite skitters and beeps high while the Penitent's monk choir groans an octave below. Every attack has its own wind-up: the Brute takes a huge breath before it slams, the Juggernaut's siren rises before each shell, the Sovereign sings a different syllable for every sword stroke, and the Penitent groans before a low censer sweep (jump it) and rings a bell before a high one (slide under it). The Hollowed variants reuse their base voice, changed: enraged ones pitched up and distorted, twinned ones doubled, haloed ones shimmering until the halo shatters.

None of it is a sound file. About 125 voices, two or three variants each, are synthesized in C++ when the game loads, in under a tenth of a second: formant filters over detuned sawtooth waves for the choir, plus a small kit of servo, vent, grind, metal and click generators. As files they would have added about 6 MB to the download above; built in code they add nothing, and the tests can measure every one of them: each must land within 2 dB of its target loudness and never clip.

More voices meant a crowded wave could turn to mush, so the mix now ranks sounds by what they're for. A director decides who gets to speak each frame: every wind-up within 40 m is heard, even out of sight, while only the nearest four enemies are allowed to chatter (idle murmurs, footsteps) and hurt sounds are rationed so a shotgun blast is one cry, not eight. In the mixer, while any wind-up is playing, the chatter steps back 8 dB and the music 3 dB, and a wind-up can take a chattering enemy's voice but never another wind-up's. Every sound's level now lives in one table, held by a test, instead of in volume numbers scattered across a hundred call sites.

The code review caught one I'd have shipped: the distortion on enraged enemies made them quieter, not more aggressive. Squashing the peaks without making the level back up cost them up to a couple of decibels exactly when they were close and loud. The drive now saturates relative to each voice's own recent peak, so it adds the same edge at any volume and changes the loudness by less than half a decibel.

### A colossus that couldn't touch you

The Penitent's rules are all relative to the floor it stands on: a low sweep hits you if your feet are within a metre of that floor, its shockwaves and burning incense hug it, and it lashes a chain at you if you perch more than 4 m above it. The tests built it standing on the pit's floor and every rule passed. In the game it was harmless. The shaft above the pit is open, so the "hard floor" for that stretch of the level is 60 m further down, and the game handed every enemy that number: as far as the Penitent knew, I was standing 60 m in the air, so every sweep passed beneath me, every hazard lay on a floor nobody could see, and the chain lashed every few seconds and never connected. A code review caught it before it shipped; the fix makes enemies in the shaft stand on whatever is really under them, the moving cage included, and a new test drives the Penitent through the same floor rule the game uses. That review also found the fight's other ways out (a band of distance where nothing could reach you, a ledge you could fight it from, a "shoot its back" weak point you could hit from the front), and each is now pinned by a test that failed first.

### A leaderboard everyone shares

Every run ends with a score: all the style you earned (which already rewards switching weapons, since the same gun over and over scores less), plus a bonus for beating par or for each wave survived, minus the damage you took, times the difficulty. A score good enough for the top ten asks for a name. On this page that goes to a shared board: a small serverless function on the site keeps each mode's top 100 in a Redis sorted set, ranked by score (the time trial by time), with a fresh board for every day's daily challenge. It cleans names to the characters the game's pixel font can draw, rejects scores and times no real run could reach, and limits each address to ten posts an hour. The daily challenge is the same run everywhere because its waves come from a seed drawn straight from the generator's raw output: C++'s random distributions are allowed to differ between standard libraries, so they'd have given different players different waves. The game itself never talks to the network: the page fetches the board into `localStorage`, where the C++ reads it exactly like its own saved board, and a finished run is handed to the page to post. Run the game anywhere without the API (a desktop build, a local server) and it simply keeps its own board.

In FAST mode you also race your own best run: its route is recorded ten times a second and played back as a glowing runner, so you can see exactly where you're gaining or losing time.

### Porting it to the browser

The same C++ runs above as WebAssembly, drawing through WebGL2. Most of the port was small because WebGL2 is OpenGL ES 3.0, and every GL call the game makes already exists there. The differences that mattered:

- **Shader dialect.** Desktop GLSL 3.30 and GLSL ES 3.00 are close, so the loader rewrites the `#version` line and adds default precision at load time, and the same `.frag` files serve both builds. ES doesn't allow implicit int-to-float conversion, so one blur shader needed explicit casts. It also bans initializers on uniforms, which the sky shader had.
- **Who owns the loop.** A browser tab can't block in `while (running)`, so the frame body moved into an `App` struct that the desktop build calls in a loop and the web build hands to `requestAnimationFrame`. The state lives on the heap because the web `main()` returns before the first frame runs.
- **Escape belongs to the browser.** Pressing Esc releases pointer lock before the game ever sees the key, so losing the lock mid-run now pauses the game. `P` pauses too.
- **The mouse can lie.** Under pointer lock, Chrome occasionally reports one enormous mouse delta, which snapped the view round 180°. The page now asks for raw input (`unadjustedMovement`) where the browser supports it, and the game drops a single delta that is many times larger than the ones around it. Two large deltas in a row are a real flick and still go through.
- **Saving.** Emscripten's filesystem lives in memory and is forgotten on reload, so settings and best times go to `localStorage` instead.
- **Shader compiles at the worst moment.** OpenGL on macOS (on Metal) and WebGL both build a GPU pipeline the first time they see a new shader and blend combination. That froze the game for about 200 ms the first time you fired, a Sentinel aimed or you scoped in. Now the first frames of a level draw one invisible instance of each effect, so those compiles happen behind the title card.
- **Escape belongs to the browser, again.** In fullscreen, the Escape you pressed to pause or close the armory also threw you out of fullscreen. Where browsers allow it (Chrome, Edge) the page now asks for a keyboard lock on Escape while fullscreen, so the game gets it and holding Escape still leaves; the pause menu has FULLSCREEN / EXIT FULLSCREEN for every browser, asking this page (which owns the iframe) through `postMessage`.
- **Real pixels.** The web build first rendered into a fixed 1280×720 canvas that the browser stretched to fit, so it always looked soft. The canvas now gets the screen pixels it actually covers (CSS size × `devicePixelRatio`), capped at the graphics quality's resolution. One catch: SDL's web port reports its window size, not the canvas's, as the drawable size, so the frame filled only the corner of a larger canvas until the final blit read the canvas size directly. The page starts on 1080p, which is gentle on laptops in a browser.

On an M2 MacBook it holds 60 fps in Chrome, the same as the native build at that refresh rate.

### What an audit turned up

Before putting the game in front of people I read it end to end, and wrote a small headless test harness for the parts that could be tested without a window. Most of what it found was invisible in a quick playtest, which is exactly why it had survived.

- **Standing on a platform flickered between grounded and airborne.** Gravity is skipped while grounded, so on the next tick the player no longer overlapped the box and read as airborne, then fell a hair and landed again. It alternated every tick, so the landing sound fired about 30 times a second. A test that stands the player on a box for 120 ticks counted 60 ground-to-air transitions. After adding a ground probe (a box top within 2 cm under the feet counts as ground) it counts zero.
- **Hitscan went through walls.** The revolver and shotgun picked the nearest enemy the ray hit and only clipped the *tracer* at the wall, so you could shoot enemies behind cover. A hit now counts only if the enemy is closer than the nearest wall along the ray.
- **A use-after-free on every menu click.** Clicking Start ran a callback that replaced the current game state, destroying the menu while its own event handler was still running. State changes are now queued and applied between frames.
- **Most of the level rendered at 15% brightness.** The world shader darkens pixels near a face's UV edge to outline boxes, but world-space UVs run past 1.0, so everywhere outside one 4 m tile counted as "on an edge". Wrapping the UVs with `fract()` fixed it, and is a big part of why the arenas now have colour.
- **Settings that did nothing.** The volume slider was saved and displayed but never reached the mixer, and the field-of-view setting was ignored on the first run because the constructor read it before it was injected.
- **Enemies stuck behind the furnace, one run in twelve.** A test sends a Ripper round the Foundry's furnace to the player. It passed, but adding an enemy shifted the random numbers and it failed: run with 60 seeds, 5 Rippers spent the whole time zig-zagging along the furnace's back wall, always moving but never getting closer. Enemies now notice when they're heading for you without closing the distance, and commit to going round one side until the straight way opens. The test runs 20 seeds and needs all 20.
- **A whole enemy type that couldn't hurt you.** Stalkers had a wind-up duration configured but no attack, so they just followed you around. They now lunge with a telegraphed melee hit and knockback.

`make test` runs the physics checks (grounding on floors and box tops, walking off ledges, jump arcs, wall blocking) and the game-logic checks (every spawn point clear of walls, every jump pad landing on its platform, each enemy type doing its attack, platforms that never clip walls, no scenery inside an arena, the weapon and XP numbers, and full simulated Arena and Fast runs) with no GPU or window.

## Results

- A complete game loop: a menu with five modes (including endless, a daily challenge and the Act II preview), four difficulties, a per-mode leaderboard, a dev level select that jumps to any arena, room or boss, settings that persist between launches (FOV, sensitivity, zoom sensitivity, volume, frame rate up to 360 Hz, graphics quality, screen shake and more), four arenas with their own lighting, fog and procedural sky, a boss fight, a time trial with splits and saved best times, XP and weapon upgrades, checkpoint retries, style scoring, a pause menu, and victory/death screens.
- Gamepad support (in the browser too), folded into the same keys the game already reads.
- A soundtrack synthesized live in the audio callback, with a track per area (and one for the Sovereign) and layers that build from the tubes into each fight. The four guns share one look and one way of moving: each glows its own colour in its accents, muzzle flash, tracers and HUD slot; ammo cells on the gun go dark as you fire and relight one by one as you reload; every gun kicks and settles by its weight, comes up from a switch with its cells booting and a chime, and reloads in the same three beats (open, feed, close) with each sound on its beat. The detailed reloads stay (the revolver's cylinder swings out, the shotgun loads shell by shell, the Lancer takes a stripper clip). Its gunshots are one family, layering the crack of a real recording with a synthesized thump, a metallic ring in the gun's pitch and a tail.
- Procedurally synthesized sound effects for most of the game, plus gunshots, footsteps, whooshes and the parry clank cut from public-domain (CC0) recordings by a script, each trimmed to start within a few milliseconds of the trigger, all played through a positional mixer with ducking and a reverb per arena. Enemy voices are synthesized at load, a machine body and a hollow choir for each type with a wind-up per attack, and a threat-aware mix keeps those wind-ups audible over everything else.
- Consistent movement across refresh rates, thanks to the fixed step and interpolation.
- Builds and runs on macOS, Windows and in the browser. It also compiles and passes its physics tests on Linux (Ubuntu 24.04), but I haven't played it on a Linux desktop, so I don't claim Linux support yet.

## What I'd do next

- **Continuous collision for the grapple.** Resolution is discrete today, so at grapple speeds a thin wall could in principle be skipped in a single tick. Sweeping the player's box along its velocity would remove that risk.
- **Touch controls for the web build.** Phones can run it but have no way to aim; twin virtual sticks would open it up to anyone visiting on mobile.
- **GPU timing.** Add timer queries per pass to see what the ten blur passes actually cost, then try a downsampled bloom chain.
